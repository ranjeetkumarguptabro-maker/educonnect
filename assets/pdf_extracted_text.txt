Ada Lovelace: A Woman Who Imagined the Future 
Ada Lovelace was a mathematician from England who lived in the 1800s. At a time 
when women had fewer opportunities to study science and mathematics, Ada became 
fascinated by numbers, machines and how they worked. 
She worked with Charles Babbage, who designed an early mechanical computer. Ada 
wrote a set of instructions for his machine that is often considered the first computer 
program. 
Ada also imagined that computers could one day do much more than calculations. She 
thought machines might work with music, pictures and other kinds of information—
ideas that were far ahead of her time. 
Ada's story teaches us that being curious and imagining new possibilities can lead to 
amazing ideas. You don't have to know exactly what the future will look like to help 
create it. 
 
Tebello Nyokong: A Scientist Who Never Gave Up 
Professor Tebello Nyokong is a famous chemist who works at Rhodes University in 
South Africa. She grew up in a rural area and faced many challenges while going to 
school. When she was younger, some people told her that science was too difficult for 
girls. At first, she studied arts, but she later decided to follow her interest in 
mathematics and science. 
Through hard work and determination, she became a leading scientist. Today, she 
researches ways to use light and special chemicals to help detect and treat cancer. 
Tebello’s story teaches us that where you come from does not decide what you can 
achieve. It is important to stay curious, believe in yourself and keep trying—even when 
something feels difficult. 
 
Chevarra Hansraj: Exploring the Universe with Maths 
Dr Chevarra Hansraj is a South African mathematician and scientist from Durban, 
KwaZulu-Natal. She became fascinated by space when she read about black holes, 
neutron stars and galaxies as a child.  
She went on to study mathematics and physics and now researches black holes, 
gravity and the universe using mathematics. Her work helps scientists understand 
what happens around some of the most extreme objects in space.

One interesting thing about Chevarra is that geometry was her least favourite topic in 
school mathematics. Today, geometry is an important part of her work!  
Chevarra's story teaches us that you don't have to love every part of a subject 
straight away. Keep asking questions, stay curious and give yourself time to discover 
what excites you. 
 
Thifhelimbilu Daphney Bucher: From Rural Limpopo to Nuclear Physics 
Dr Thifhelimbilu Daphney Bucher is a South African nuclear physicist who grew up in 
rural Limpopo. Her family did not have much money, but her mother worked hard to 
help her get an education.  
Daphney became fascinated by physics and worked hard to study it. She went on to 
earn a PhD in experimental nuclear physics and became a researcher at South 
Africa's iThemba LABS, where scientists study the tiny particles that make up atomic 
nuclei.  
Today, she continues to research how atomic nuclei behave and teaches the next 
generation of students.  
Daphney's story teaches us that your circumstances do not have to decide your 
future. With curiosity, hard work and support, you can follow your interests and 
discover how far they can take you. 
 
Seymour Papert: A South African Who Changed How Children Learn 
Seymour Papert was a mathematician and computer scientist who was born in 
Pretoria, South Africa. He became fascinated by mathematics and computers and 
believed that children should learn by exploring, experimenting and making things. 
Papert helped create Logo, a computer programming language that allowed children to 
give instructions to a small turtle on the screen. By using simple commands, children 
could create shapes, pictures and patterns while learning mathematics and 
programming. 
His ideas helped change the way people think about children, computers and

Katherine Johnson: The Mathematician Who Helped Reach Space 
Katherine Johnson was an American mathematician who loved numbers and solving 
problems from a young age. At a time when opportunities for Black women were 
limited, she worked hard to become a mathematician at NASA. 
Katherine used mathematics to calculate the paths of spacecraft travelling through 
space. Her calculations helped astronauts travel safely, including John Glenn's historic 
trip around Earth. 
She was known for being very careful with her work and never being afraid to ask 
questions and check the answers. Her mathematics helped NASA make important 
decisions about space missions. 
Katherine's story teaches us that maths can be much bigger than a classroom. With 
curiosity, determination and careful thinking, the skills you learn in maths can help 
solve some of the world's biggest problems. 
 
Maggie Aphane: A Mathematician Breaking Barriers 
Professor Maggie Aphane is a South African mathematician who studies topology, a 
part of mathematics that explores shapes, spaces and how they are connected. She 
earned her degrees in mathematics from the University of South Africa (UNISA), 
including her PhD in 2016.  
Maggie became the first South African woman and second woman overall to earn a 
PhD in Mathematics from UNISA. She has also spoken about sometimes being the 
only Black woman at international mathematics conferences.  
Today, she is a mathematics professor and Head of the Department of Mathematics and 
Applied Mathematics at Sefako Makgatho Health Sciences University. Her research 
connects mathematics with areas such as data science, networks and medical 
imaging.  
Maggie's story teaches us: You don't have to see many people like yourself in a subject 
to belong there. Keep learning, ask questions and make your own path in maths. 
 
Allan McLeod Cormack: The Mathematician Who Helped Us See Inside the Body 
Allan McLeod Cormack was a South African physicist and mathematician born in 
Johannesburg. He became interested in using mathematics and physics to understand 
how things work. 

=== PAGE 4 ===
Cormack developed mathematical ideas that showed how X-rays taken from different 
angles could be used to create pictures of what is inside the body. These ideas 
became an important part of the science behind CT scans.  
In 1979, Cormack and British engineer Godfrey Hounsfield were awarded the Nobel 
Prize in Physiology or Medicine for their work in developing computer-assisted 
tomography.  
Cormack's story teaches us: Mathematics is not only about numbers on a page. 
Maths can help us understand the world, solve difficult problems and even improve 
people's health. 
 
Marie Curie: The Scientist Who Changed Our Understanding of Atoms 
Marie Curie was a scientist born in Poland who later moved to France to study and do 
research. She loved learning about mathematics and physics, even when women had 
fewer opportunities to study science. 
Marie and her husband, Pierre Curie, researched radioactivity, a term Marie helped 
develop. She discovered two new elements, polonium and radium, and studied how 
radiation could be used in science and medicine. 
Marie became the first woman to win a Nobel Prize and the first person to win Nobel 
Prizes in two different scientific fields: Physics and Chemistry. 
Marie’s story teaches us: You can make important discoveries by being curious, 
patient and willing to keep exploring difficult questions. Your curiosity can take you 
further than you imagine.
